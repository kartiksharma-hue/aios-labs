What Makes a Good Landing Page?

Short answer

One goal, a headline that continues the promise made by the ad that brought the visitor, immediate clarity on what is offered and to whom, proof positioned beside the claims it supports, objections answered in the order they arise, minimal form friction, and fast loading on a mid-range mobile phone. Most underperforming pages fail at least three of these.

RSKartik SharmaPerformance Marketer · ₹50Cr+ ad spend managed

Published 2026-09-05 · Updated 2026-09-07

One page, one job

A landing page that offers a demo, a newsletter, a download and a phone call converts worse on all four than a page that offers one. Every additional option adds a decision, and decisions cost conversions.

This is the main structural difference between a landing page and a homepage. A homepage serves many audiences with many intents. A campaign visitor arrived with exactly one, set by the ad they clicked.

Message match

The visitor was promised something specific. If the page opens with a generic brand statement instead of continuing that promise, they experience a mismatch, a moment of did I click the wrong thing, and a meaningful share leave immediately.

Practically: the headline should echo the ad's core phrase, the visual language should carry over, and the offer should be identical. If the ad said forty percent off winter jackets, the page should say forty percent off winter jackets, not welcome to our store.

The first screen on a phone

Assume the visitor sees only the first screen on a mid-range phone and decides from that whether to continue. Four things need to be in it: what this is, who it is for, why it is worth their attention, and one obvious action.

Large hero images that push the value proposition below the fold are the most common way this fails. So are headlines that describe the company rather than the offer.

Objections in the order they occur

A visitor's doubts arrive in a predictable sequence, and the page should answer them in that order rather than in whatever order the sections were designed.

  • Does this actually work?: proof, results, demonstration
  • Would it work for someone like me?: specificity, use cases, relatable examples
  • What does it cost?: pricing or a clear indication of range
  • What if it goes wrong?: guarantee, returns, support
  • What happens next?: an explicit description of the next step

Proof placement

Proof works when it sits next to the claim it supports. A testimonial about fast delivery belongs beside the delivery promise, not in a carousel of five testimonials halfway down the page.

Specific proof outperforms general proof by a wide margin. Increased revenue by 34 percent in four months is credible; great service, highly recommended is noise. Screenshots, numbers and named sources do work that adjectives cannot.

Form friction

Every field costs completions. Ask only what sales genuinely needs to make the first contact, usually name and one contact method. Qualification questions can come after the contact details are captured, or on the thank-you page, so a partial answer still leaves you a reachable lead.

On mobile, set input types correctly so the right keyboard appears, validate inline rather than on submit, and never clear a filled form on an error. These are small details that measurably change completion rates.

Speed

A landing page receiving paid traffic should be interactive in about a second on mobile data. Every second of delay costs conversions, and you are paying for each of those visitors twice: once for the click, once in the lost sale.

Landing pages are also where speed is easiest to control, because there is no legacy theme, no plugin sprawl and no requirement to serve ten other purposes.

Answers

Related questions

Should a landing page have navigation?

Usually not, or only a logo. Navigation gives the visitor exits from a page whose entire purpose is a single action. The exception is high-consideration purchases where people genuinely need to research the company before converting.

How long should a landing page be?

As long as it takes to answer every objection and no longer. Low-cost, low-risk offers convert on short pages. High-ticket or unfamiliar offers need length because there are more doubts to resolve. Length is a consequence of the offer, not a style choice.

Should we use a video on the landing page?

Only if it earns its place. Video can explain a complex product quickly, but it also delays the page and many visitors will not play it. If you use one, the page must still work completely with the video ignored.

How many CTAs should there be?

One action, repeated as often as the page length warrants. A long page should offer the same button several times so the visitor never has to scroll back. Multiple different actions is the problem; multiple instances of the same action is good practice.
